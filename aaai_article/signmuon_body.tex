\begin{abstract}\label{sec:abs}
SignMuon, the sign compression of the Muon update to one bit per parameter, is the most straightforward adaptation of Muon to an extremely low communication budget. It outperforms SignSGD in practice, yet it can diverge on a linear function. The natural conjecture attributes this to the order of operations, and holds that signing before rather than after the Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) would remedy it. It does not: sign-before (MuonUSign) likewise diverges, as does signing on both sides (MuonSign), so that no naive placement of the sign around the LMO converges. Error feedback repairs one of them: EF21-MuonUSign attains the standard $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})$ rate for smooth nonconvex optimization, and compressing the downlink as well exacts little beyond that. What the guarantee does exact is the placement. Across centralized, federated and language-modelling experiments the leading method is consistently sign-\emph{after}-the-LMO, precisely the placement our counterexamples defeat and error feedback fails to repair; the provably convergent variants trail it. We document this tension rather than resolve it: on random instances the gradient/step alignment our counterexamples annihilate remains positive, so they constrain worst-case problems and not CIFAR-10.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Training a deep network across clients consumes bandwidth as well as computation: every round each client transmits a full update, and under a limited link those updates dominate the wall-clock cost. Compression is the standard remedy \cite{bernstein2018signsgd, bernstein2019signsgdmajority, beznosikov2020biased}, and SignSGD is its extreme point: one bit per coordinate, at slight cost in accuracy. SignSGD, however, flattens each weight matrix into a vector, discarding the structure matrix-aware optimizers exploit. Muon exploits precisely that structure, orthonormalizing the momentum matrix before stepping, which stabilizes training and in several settings surpasses tuned adaptive methods \cite{jordan2024muon, bernstein2024old, shah2025practical}. What it transmits is a \emph{dense} matrix at full precision, the one commodity a bandwidth-limited system cannot afford. Matrix geometry and a one-bit budget therefore pull in opposite directions.

% In this work, we propose SignMuon,
% % \footnote{Code is available at: \url{https://github.com/intsystems/2026-Project-203/tree/main}},
% an optimization algorithm that applies sign compression to the Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) update of Muon. SignSGD transmits the sign of the coordinates of the stochastic gradient direction. Our algorithm first computes an update direction using LMO, and then transmits only the sign of this direction. This approach allows for the matrix structure of the parameters to be accounted for and preserves the advantages of Muon, while reducing the amount of transmitted information to one bit per coordinate. Consequently, SignMuon combines the fast convergence of structured optimizers with the communication efficiency of sign-based approaches.
We study the natural ways to combine sign compression with the Muon LMO at one bit per parameter: \textbf{SignMuon}, which signs \emph{after} the LMO, $\operatorname{sign}(\operatorname{LMO}(\cdot))$; \textbf{MuonUSign}, which signs \emph{before}, $\operatorname{LMO}(\operatorname{sign}(\cdot))$; and \textbf{MuonSign}, which signs on \emph{both} sides and so compresses uplink and downlink alike. All three retain Muon's matrix-aware geometry, but only sign-after attains Muon's accuracy: in federation the three span several accuracy points, in the order after, before, both sides, the last falling below SignSGD.

None of them, however, has a global convergence guarantee. We prove that all three can diverge: SignMuon on a $4\times4$ instance (Theorem~\ref{th:1}), MuonUSign and MuonSign on a single common $5\times5$ one (Theorems~\ref{th:2}--\ref{th:3}); in each case the 1-bit step becomes an ascent direction on a linear objective. To restore convergence at the same communication volume we introduce \textbf{EF21-MuonUSign}, adapted from EF21-Muon~\citep{gruntkowska2025error}, which error-feedbacks the \emph{gradient} rather than the oracle's output, and \textbf{EF21-MuonSign}, which adds a second loop on the downlink so that both channels are one bit. Both descend on the counterexamples above.

% The effectiveness of the proposed optimizer is evaluated on synthetic and practical tasks: centralized image classification on CIFAR-10 with a ResNet-18 backbone, federated image classification on CIFAR-10 with a CNN across several client counts, and on a synthetic smooth convex least-squares problem.
We evaluate on centralized CIFAR-10 with a ResNet-18, federated CIFAR-10 at $N=11$ clients, the nanoGPT speedrun, and a synthetic convex quadratic. Our contributions are: (i)~the three placements, as matrix-aware 1-bit optimizers; (ii)~counterexamples proving all three may diverge (Theorems~\ref{th:1}--\ref{th:3}), at minimal matrix sizes; (iii)~a universal divergence theorem (Theorem~\ref{th:ef_div}): error feedback on the LMO \emph{output} repairs SignMuon for no $(L,\eta,\mu)$; (iv)~EF21-MuonUSign and EF21-MuonSign, restoring the guarantee at a one-bit uplink and at one bit in both directions respectively; and (v)~a tuned five-seed federated comparison of all six, which separates the three placements that single-seed evidence had shown as coincident and locates the guarantee's cost at the placement, not the downlink. Proofs, algorithms and every numbered result cited but not stated here are in the supplementary material, which continues this numbering; each citation names its appendix section.

% Experimental results show that applying sign compression to Muon directions reduces communication overhead (by $32\times$) while maintaining optimization rates. In the centralized setting, SignMuon consistently outperforms the baseline SignSGD method in both convergence speed and final accuracy. At the same time, the accuracy of the proposed 1-bit method is only $1-1.5\%$ lower than that of the Muon optimizer, while significantly outperforming basic signed algorithms. In federated setting, integrating SignMuon with client momentum and Majority Vote aggregation method provides stable convergence and robustness to anomalous client updates even under severe communication constraints. Furthermore, we present an analytical example that formalizes the limitations of applying sign compression to LMO-based updates in the deterministic setting and for smooth convex optimization problems. The results of our experiments demonstrate that the proposed algorithm successfully combines LMO-based methods with sign compression without any significant loss in training accuracy.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:relWork}
\paragraph{Sign compression.} SignSGD \citep{bernstein2018signsgd}, extended to federated learning by majority vote \citep{bernstein2019signsgdmajority}, needs growing batches to converge; its bias is otherwise repaired by error feedback \citep{karimireddy2019efsign}, sharpened into EF21 \citep{richtarik2021ef21} and its bidirectional form EF21-P \citep{gruntkowska2023ef21}, by momentum \citep{cutkosky2020momentum,sun2023signmomentum}, or by randomizing the sign operator \citep{chen2020median,safaryan2021ssdm,jin2024stosign}. Coarser quantizers with unbiasedness or variance reduction are the other branch \citep{alistarh2017qsgd,horvath2023stochastic,beznosikov2020biased}.

\paragraph{LMO optimizers and their limits.} Muon \citep{jordan2024muon} is the spectral-norm instance of a norm-constrained LMO step \citep{bernstein2024old,pethick2025training}, analysed by \citet{li2025note} and \citet{kovalev2025understanding} and generalized to arbitrary layer norms by Gluon \citep{riabinin2025gluon}; \citet{crawshaw2025exploration} survey the resulting family. Negative results exist for the uncompressed method too: \citet{parshakova2026muondoesconvergeconvex} show Muon need not converge on convex Lipschitz problems, and \citet{shulgin2025inexactmuon} quantify the cost of an inexact oracle. Ours are of the same kind, but caused by the compressor rather than by the geometry.

\paragraph{Compressed and federated Muon.} \citet{gruntkowska2025error} give the error-feedback framework for bidirectionally compressed Muon/Gluon that our positive result reduces to; \citet{qian2026communicationefficientgluon} add variance reduction, \citet{takezawa2025fedmuonfederatedlearningbiascorrected} and \citet{zhang2025fedmuon} study federated LMO steps without compression, \citet{ahn2025dion} and \citet{gupta2025effective} compress the oracle and its state respectively, and \citet{therien2025muloco} report that Muon-produced updates compress well in practice. Concurrently, \citet{mishra2026signmuon} proposed plain SignMuon with an $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ guarantee; Appendix~\ref{app:mishra} establishes that their finalized rate covers only the gradient-sign instantiation, leaving the polar-sign method uninformative on precisely the instances of Theorem~\ref{th:1}. Two further methods share our names but not our object: \citet{bolatov2026lionmuon} \emph{alternate} spectral and sign steps rather than composing them, and \citet{kravatskiy2025kyfannorms} mix the geometries \emph{inside} the oracle, so that S-Muon remains an LMO for an explicit norm and retains the attendant theory. Our placements situate the sign \emph{around} the oracle, where it constitutes a compressor and not a geometry, so the counterexamples below bear upon neither (Appendix~\ref{app:smuon}).

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:proState}

We consider the stochastic optimization problem
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{X} \in \mathcal{X}} \{f(\mathbf{X}) := \mathbb{E}_{\xi \sim \mathcal{D}} [f_{\xi}(\mathbf{X})]\},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{X}$ is the parameter space (e.g., $\mathbb{R}^d$ or $\mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$) and the $f_{\xi} : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ are continuously differentiable and possibly non-convex: $f_{\xi}(\mathbf{X})$ is the loss of a model $\mathbf{X}$ at a data point $\xi \sim \mathcal{D}$. In the federated setting the data are split across clients, and the training problem becomes

\begin{multline}
\min_{\mathbf{X} \in \mathcal{X}} \left\{ f(\mathbf{X}) := \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N f_j(\mathbf{X})\right\}, \\
f_j(\mathbf{X})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_j\sim\mathcal{D}_j}[f_j(\mathbf{X};\xi_j)]
\label{fed_eq}
\end{multline}
where $N \ge 1$ is the number of clients and $f_j$ is the loss on the data $\mathcal{D}_j$ held by client $j \in [N] := \{1, \dots, N\}$.

Two assumptions run through the paper. The model is a tuple of matrix layers $\mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{X}_1,\dots,\mathbf{X}_p]$, $\mathbf{X}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{m_i\times n_i}$, with $\nabla_i$ the gradient block of layer $i$. Each layer carries the spectral norm $\|\cdot\|_{2\to2}$, whose dual is the nuclear norm $\|\cdot\|_{*}$, and we write $\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X})\|_*^2 := \sum_i\|\nabla_i f(\mathbf{X})\|_*^2$; a single matrix parameter is the case $p=1$.

\begin{assumption}[\textbf{Lower boundedness}]\label{as:1}
$f(\mathbf{X}) \ge f^*$ for all $\mathbf{X}$; where explicitly invoked, each local $f_j \ge f_j^*$ as well.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[\textbf{Layer-wise smoothness}]\label{as:2}
For every layer $i$ and all $\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}$,
\begin{equation}
\|\nabla_i g(\mathbf{X}) - \nabla_i g(\mathbf{Y})\|_{*} \le L_i^{g}\,\|\mathbf{X}_i - \mathbf{Y}_i\|_{2\to 2}
\end{equation}
for $g = f$ (constant $L_i$) and $g = f_j$ (constant $L_{i,j}$). A weaker layer-wise $(L^0,L^1)$ form, stated in Appendix~\ref{app:prelim}, is used only in Corollary~\ref{cor:l0l1} (Appendix~\ref{app:hyp}).
\end{assumption}

A Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) minimizes a linear form over the unit ball of a norm, returning the steepest-descent direction in that norm (Appendix~\ref{app:prelim}). Muon takes that norm to be spectral on each matrix layer, which turns the oracle into an orthogonalization: writing $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top$ for the rank-$r$ singular value decomposition of $\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, the LMO direction is
\begin{equation}
A(\mathbf{M})=-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n},
\qquad \operatorname{polar}(\mathbf{M}):=-A(\mathbf{M}).
\end{equation}
In practice the oracle is applied to a momentum estimate rather than to $\mathbf{G}_t$ itself; \eqref{eq:signa_general} below gives the full iteration. What Muon must transmit is the dense $\mathbf{D}_t$, precisely what a bandwidth-limited link cannot accommodate; our object is therefore its matrix-aware geometry at a one-bit budget.


\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}
\paragraph{From SignSGD to Sign\,A.} Every method in the SignSGD lineage signs a \emph{gradient}, but nothing in the construction requires it: any optimizer emitting a direction can have that direction signed instead, and should inherit whatever the direction was contributing. For LMO-based optimizers the expectation is more than an analogy, because their theory is uniform in the geometry: one analysis covers every norm, which enters only through its oracle and a pair of norm-equivalence constants \citep{pethick2025training,kovalev2025understanding,riabinin2025gluon}. If signing a direction were harmless it should be harmless across that family, and the member one actually seeks to compress is Muon.

A second reason to expect it to work is the one that misleads. The sign step is not merely compatible with the LMO view; it \emph{is} an LMO, steepest descent in $\ell_\infty$ \citep{bernstein2024old}, so signing a Muon direction composes two oracles, each sound alone. The composition is an LMO for \emph{no} norm. An oracle for a norm $\|\cdot\|$ returns the direction $\mathbf{D}=-A(\mathbf{G})$, for which $\langle\mathbf{G},\mathbf{D}\rangle=\max_{\|\mathbf{Y}\|\le1}\langle\mathbf{G},\mathbf{Y}\rangle=\|\mathbf{G}\|_{\mathrm{dual}}$, nonnegative because $\mathbf{0}$ is feasible; Theorems~\ref{th:1}--\ref{th:3} exhibit gradients driving that inner product strictly negative. The guarantee is forfeited in the composition, not in either factor.

\paragraph{The Sign\,A family.} Let A be any optimizer built on LMO directions. A Sign\,A method initializes $\mathbf{M}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ and at each iteration $t \ge 1$ performs
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:signa_general}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{M}_t &= \mu \mathbf{M}_{t-1} + (1-\mu)\,\mathbf{G}_t,\\
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t &= \mathbf{M}_t
   \ \ \text{or}\ \ (1-\mu)\mathbf{G}_t + \mu \mathbf{M}_t\ \ \text{(Nesterov)},\\
\mathbf{D}_t &= -A(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t),
   \qquad \mathbf{s}_t = \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{D}_t) \in \{\pm 1\}^{m \times n},\\
\mathbf{X}_{t} &= \mathbf{X}_{t-1} - \eta_t \mathbf{s}_t,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is the parameter matrix, $\mathbf{G}_t$ a stochastic gradient evaluated at $\mathbf{X}_{t-1}$, $\mu \in [0, 1)$ the momentum coefficient and $\eta_t$ the learning rate; $A(\cdot)$ is the LMO, and $\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)$ acts element-wise on the structured direction $\mathbf{D}_t$, with $\operatorname{sign}(0)$ resolved to an independent random $\pm1$ so that the transmitted alphabet stays binary. Momentum is in exponential-moving-average form throughout; the heavy-ball form differs by the positive factor $1/(1-\mu)$, which $\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)$ and the LMO both absorb, so no result below distinguishes them.

Our three methods are the three placements of the sign around this oracle. They share the update $\mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{X}_{t-1}-\eta_t\,\mathbf{s}_t$ and differ only in where the sign acts on $\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:three_placements}
\mathbf{s}_t=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{sign}\!\bigl(\operatorname{polar}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t)\bigr) & \text{(SignMuon: after)},\\[2pt]
\operatorname{polar}\!\bigl(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t)\bigr) & \text{(MuonUSign: before)},\\[2pt]
\operatorname{sign}\!\bigl(\operatorname{polar}(\operatorname{sign}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t))\bigr) & \text{(MuonSign: both)}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The three are Algorithms~\ref{central_alg},~\ref{alg:muon_usign} and~\ref{alg:muon_sign} (Appendix~\ref{app:alg}), all transmitting one bit per parameter on the uplink. On the downlink SignMuon and MuonSign distribute a $\pm1$-valued object and so cost one bit in each direction, whereas MuonUSign's server-side $\operatorname{polar}(\cdot)$ is dense and is transmitted at full precision.

\subsection{Centralized Learning}
% In the centralized setting, SignMuon is applied to the matrix-valued parameters of a neural network. The practical implementation takes into account the tensor structure of model parameters. The optimization process is divided into two separate streams: matrix parameters (weights of convolutional and fully connected layers, $n_{dim} \ge 2$) are updated using the SignMuon LMO-based procedure, whereas vector parameters (bias, Batch Normalization layer, $n_{dim} = 1$) as well as the final classification layer are optimized using the standard AdamW optimizer. This design was originally introduced in the empirical description of Muon by \citet{jordan2024muon}. The first theoretical convergence result was derived by Li and Hong, who analyzed the smooth non-convex setting \cite{li2025note} and subsequently formalized within the Gluon framework \cite{riabinin2025gluon}.
Centrally, SignMuon is applied to the matrix-valued parameters (convolutional and fully connected weights, $n_{dim}\ge 2$), while vector parameters (biases, BatchNorm) and the final classification layer are trained with AdamW, the design of \citet{jordan2024muon}, formalized in \cite{li2025note, riabinin2025gluon}.

We approximate $\operatorname{polar}(\cdot)$ by a 5th-order Newton--Schulz iteration rather than a full SVD (Algorithm~\ref{alg:muon_lmo}). One further implementation choice has consequences for every experiment below.

\paragraph{Per-layer step sizes: the unit-gain heuristic.} The methods of this paper produce step matrices from two families with \emph{exactly} known Frobenius norms: $\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^\top\|_F=\sqrt{r}$ for the \textsc{lmo}-terminated methods ($r=\min(m,n)$), and $\|\mathbf{s}\|_F=\sqrt{mn}$ for the \textsc{sign}-terminated ones. These scale differently with layer shape, so no single global $\eta$ is appropriate for both families, or across layers within one. We fix the shape dependence a priori and tune only a shape-free base rate $\eta_0$, setting $\eta_\ell=\eta_0\lambda_\ell$ with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:unit_gain_main}
\lambda_\ell=\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\|\mathbf{s}_\ell\|_F}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
\lambda_\ell^{\textsc{lmo}}=\sqrt{\max(1,m/n)},\\[2pt]
\lambda_\ell^{\textsc{sign}}=1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The criterion is that every layer's update possess the same root-mean-square input--output gain, the average-case form of the spectral scaling condition \citep{yang2023spectral,large2024modular}, so that $\eta_0$ is precisely the per-step RMS gain. We treat \eqref{eq:unit_gain_main} as a heuristic and apply it uniformly for one reason above all: its \textsc{lmo} branch \emph{reproduces} the aspect-ratio factor already present in the reference Muon implementation \citep{jordan2024muon}, so that a criterion accepted for one family supplies the missing counterpart for the other. Appendix~\ref{app:lrscale} gives the derivation and the measurement selecting the exponent $\tfrac12$ over $\mu$P's $1$.

\subsection{Theoretical limitations}
The Muon LMO direction, steepest descent in the spectral norm, does not survive sign compression, before or after the oracle: in each of the three placements the compressed step can become an \emph{ascent} direction on a smooth objective. We read $\operatorname{polar}(\cdot)$ off the rank-truncated SVD; at a rank-deficient argument the spectral-ball LMO is not unique, and this selection, the one our implementation makes, is part of what the counterexamples assert.

We refute the descent property on \emph{linear} objectives, the simplest smooth functions:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lin_obj}
f(\mathbf{W})=\langle \mathbf{G},\mathbf{W}\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{G}^\top\mathbf{W}),\qquad \nabla f(\mathbf{W})\equiv \mathbf{G}.
\end{equation}
Gradient descent and full-precision Muon drive $f\to-\infty$ here, so a rule that instead drives $f\to+\infty$ is unambiguously ascending (Remark~\ref{rem:efsm_bdd}, Appendix~\ref{app:proof_th4}, restores Assumption~\ref{as:1} without moving any trajectory below).

On \eqref{eq:lin_obj} the step is the constant matrix $\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{G})$ whatever the momentum, so momentum affects neither convergence nor divergence (Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}, Appendix~\ref{app:proof_reduction}) and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lin_increment}
f(\mathbf{X}_{t})-f(\mathbf{X}_{t-1})=-\eta_t\,\bigl\langle \mathbf{G},\ \mathbf{s}(\mathbf{G})\bigr\rangle
\end{equation}
for every $\mu\in[0,1)$ and either momentum rule. One gradient with $\langle\mathbf{G},\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{G})\rangle<0$ thus makes $f$ increase at \emph{every} step, at \emph{any} step size, with $f(\mathbf{X}_t)\to+\infty$ whenever $\sum_t\eta_t=\infty$. Such a gradient exists for every placement, at small size.

\begin{informalthm}{Theorems~\ref{th:1}--\ref{th:3} (informal)}
SignMuon, MuonUSign and MuonSign all diverge. There is a $4\times4$ gradient on which SignMuon ascends, and a single $5\times5$ gradient on which MuonUSign and MuonSign both ascend: on the corresponding linear objective~\eqref{eq:lin_obj} each method increases $f$ at every iteration, for every step size $\eta_t>0$, every $\mu\in[0,1)$ and either momentum rule, with $f(\mathbf{X}_t)\to+\infty$ whenever $\sum_t\eta_t=\infty$.
\end{informalthm}

The instances are explicit and the inner products $\langle\mathbf{G},\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{G})\rangle$ exact rationals; Appendices~\ref{app:proof_th1}--\ref{app:proof_th3} state and prove the theorems and delimit the two shapes from below. For MuonUSign and MuonSign the bound is exhaustive: no shape smaller than $4\times5$ admits an ascending instance, and $5\times5$ is the smallest square one. For SignMuon, $2\times2$ is excluded by proof and $3\times3$ by an unsuccessful search. The left and centre panels of Figure~\ref{fig:divergence_plot} exhibit the ascents.

\subsection{Centralized Error Feedback: EF21-SignMuon}

Theorems~\ref{th:1}--\ref{th:3} rule out every static placement. The classical remedy for a biased one-bit compressor is \emph{error feedback}: store the compression error and reinject it at the next step, which repairs SignSGD~\cite{karimireddy2019efsign} and was sharpened into EF21~\cite{richtarik2021ef21}. Its most direct use for SignMuon keeps the geometry (the sign \emph{after} the LMO) and error-feedbacks the oracle's output. The resulting method, \textbf{EF21-SignMuon} (Algorithm~\ref{ef21_signmuon}), tracks an estimate $\mathbf{d}_t^{\mathrm{est}}$ of the LMO direction $\mathbf{D}_t=\operatorname{polar}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t)$, refreshed by a scaled sign of the residual,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:efsm_est}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{d}_t^{\mathrm{est}}&=\mathbf{d}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{est}}+\alpha_t\operatorname{sign}\!\bigl(\mathbf{D}_t-\mathbf{d}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{est}}\bigr),\\
\alpha_t&=\operatorname{mean}\bigl|\mathbf{D}_t-\mathbf{d}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{est}}\bigr|,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and steps $\mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{X}_{t-1}-\eta_t\,\mathbf{d}_t^{\mathrm{est}}$.

Error feedback on the compressed \emph{output}, however, does not restore the descent property, and fails for \emph{every} step size and momentum setting.
% The three divergence theorems are now stated in the appendix, next to their
% proofs, but the paper's numbering is fixed by everything that cites it (the
% code, its READMEs, the reproduction guide): Theorems 1-3 are the three sign
% placements, 4 is this one, 5 is the convergence result. The counter is set by
% hand here and reset in the appendix to keep those numbers.
\setcounter{theorem}{3}
\begin{theorem}[Divergence of EF21-SignMuon]\label{th:ef_div}
For every $L>0$, step size $\eta>0$, momentum coefficient $\mu\in[0,1)$, and either momentum variant, there is an $L$-smooth (Assumption~\ref{as:2}), bounded-below (Assumption~\ref{as:1}) function $f:\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}\!\to\mathbb{R}$ on which EF21-SignMuon started at $\mathbf{X}_0=\mathbf{0}$ diverges: for an explicit constant $c=c(f)>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact_rate}
f(\mathbf{X}_{t+2})-f(\mathbf{X}_t)=c\,L\eta^2>0\qquad(t\ge3),
\end{equation}
so $f(\mathbf{X}_t)\to+\infty$. In particular, no step-size rule $\eta=\eta(L,\mu)$ using only the smoothness and momentum constants can make the method convergent.
\end{theorem}
The obstruction (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof_th4}) is specific to signing the LMO output: a \emph{single} magnitude $\alpha_t$ rescales all entries' signs at once, so a large, sign-flipping off-diagonal forces $\alpha_t=\Theta(1)$ and overwhelms the small diagonal target, whose estimate then locks onto the \emph{wrong} sign and drives one coordinate of $\mathbf{X}_t$ to infinity. Momentum affords no remedy: Figure~\ref{fig:ef21_momentum} (Appendix~\ref{app:proof_th4}) records the same rate for every $\mu$ and both variants.

\subsection{Centralized Error Feedback: EF21-MuonUSign and EF21-MuonSign}
The defect is not the shared magnitude $\alpha_t$ as such, since the methods below couple all coordinates through the same scalar; it is \emph{what the estimator is asked to track}. Error feedback requires a target that settles, whereas the LMO output $\operatorname{polar}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t)$ is non-Lipschitz in the momentum and can jump by $\Theta(1)$ between rounds however small $\eta$ is. Compressing the \emph{gradient} instead leaves the estimator tracking a target that moves with the step size, which restores convergence at the same one-bit uplink cost; we adapt the EF21-Muon mechanism~\cite{gruntkowska2025error} to sign compression.

The \textbf{USign} compressor is the directional pair $(\mathcal{C}_{\uparrow},\mathcal{C}_{\downarrow})$ with $\mathcal{C}_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{Y}) = \operatorname{mean}|\mathbf{Y}|\,\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{Y})$ the scaled sign on the uplink and $\mathcal{C}_{\downarrow} = I$ the identity on the downlink: one bit per parameter from client to server, a full-precision model back. \textbf{EF21-MuonUSign} pairs it with EF21 and the Muon LMO, maintaining a gradient estimator $\mathbf{g}_t^{\mathrm{est}}$ refreshed by a contractive sign compressor applied to the residual $\Delta_t = \tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t - \mathbf{g}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{est}}$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_t = \operatorname{mean}(|\Delta_t|),\quad
\mathbf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{est}} = \mathbf{g}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{est}} + \alpha_t\,\operatorname{sign}(\Delta_t),
\label{eq:ef21_central}
\end{equation}
and takes the descent step
\begin{multline}
\mathbf{X}_{t} = \mathbf{X}_{t-1} - \eta_t\,\,\mathbf{D}_t,
\\ \mathbf{D}_t = -A(\mathbf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{est}}) \approx \mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{V}^\top_{t}.
\label{eq:ef_step}
\end{multline}
Here $\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{V}_{t}^\top$ is the polar factor of the \emph{refreshed} estimator, not of $\nabla f$, so no step-wise descent property is claimed, only the tracking hypothesis the guarantee requires. The sign in~\eqref{eq:ef21_central} acts on the internal compression residual alone, so the uplink remains at one bit per parameter (Algorithm~\ref{central_alg_ef}, Appendix~\ref{app:alg}). Figure~\ref{fig:divergence_plot} confirms that EF21-MuonUSign descends on the counterexample on which SignMuon ascends.

That hypothesis is the sole remaining requirement. Appendix~\ref{app:hyp} shows both error-feedback methods to be \emph{exact instances} of the EF21-Muon framework of \citet{gruntkowska2025error}, the one substantive verification being that the scaled sign is a contractive compressor (Lemma~\ref{lem:signcontr}), and so to inherit its guarantees (Theorem~\ref{thm:conv}): the standard $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})$ rate for $\min_{t\le T}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{X}_t)\|_*^2$ under Assumptions~\ref{as:1}--\ref{as:2}, and, for EF21-MuonUSign at a plain constant step size, $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/4})$ for $\min_t\sum_i\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_i f(\mathbf{X}_t)\|_*$ under $(L^0,L^1)$-smoothness (Corollaries~\ref{cor:smooth}--\ref{cor:l0l1}, which also give the per-layer weighted form).

\textbf{EF21-MuonSign} makes both channels one-bit by applying the same scaled-sign operator to the downlink. The mechanism is a second error-feedback loop, on the \emph{model} rather than the gradient, and it splits the method into two iterates:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two_models}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{X}_{t}&=\mathbf{X}_{t-1}-\eta_t\mathbf{D}_t &&\text{(server model)},\\
  \mathbf{W}_{t}&=\mathbf{W}_{t-1}+\alpha_t^{\downarrow}\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{X}_{t}-\mathbf{W}_{t-1}) &&\text{(broadcast model)},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{D}_t=-A(\mathbf{g}_{t}^{\mathrm{est}})$ as before, and $\alpha_t^{\downarrow}=\operatorname{mean}|\mathbf{X}_t-\mathbf{W}_{t-1}|$. The server model $\mathbf{X}$ takes the exact LMO step and never leaves the server; what crosses the downlink is the one-bit refresh of $\mathbf{W}$, the only model the remainder of the method observes, since gradients, momentum and the uplink residual are all computed there, whereas the guarantee bounds the gradient at $\mathbf{X}$. Under an exact downlink the two coincide and the method reduces to EF21-MuonUSign. The $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})$ rate survives the second loop, though not without charge: a sign-based downlink is contractive in the Euclidean norm, whereas the spectral geometry requires contractivity in the layer norm, so the admissible step size shrinks by a further $\sqrt{r}$ in the layer rank. This is no artefact of the analysis, the scaled sign being spectrally contractive for \emph{no} parameter at all, so that only a different compressor eliminates it (Remark~\ref{rem:normmismatch}, Appendix~\ref{app:hyp}). Section~\ref{sec:exp_nanogpt} quantifies the resulting expense; the full procedure is Algorithm~\ref{central_alg_ud}. Both error-feedback methods incur a further charge in memory, one buffer per compressed channel: negligible on the server, potentially prohibitive on memory-limited workers.


\subsection{Federated Learning}
In the federated setting~\eqref{fed_eq} each client computes a stochastic gradient at the broadcast model, maintains a momentum buffer, applies the Muon LMO (Algorithm~\ref{alg:muon_lmo}), and uploads the elementwise sign of the result; the server takes a majority vote, $\mathbf{s}_t^{\mathrm{agg}}=\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\sum_{j} \mathbf{s}_t^{(j)}\bigr)$, and sets $\mathbf{X}_t=\mathbf{X}_{t-1}-\eta_t\mathbf{s}_t^{\mathrm{agg}}$. Both directions then cost one bit per parameter: $\mathbf{s}_t^{\mathrm{agg}}$ is $\pm1$-valued in every coordinate, exactly so at an odd client count, whence the server broadcasts the \emph{vote} rather than the model, and replicas started from a common $\mathbf{X}_0$ never drift. This is a property of the vote, not of the compressor \citep{bernstein2019signsgdmajority}.

The error-feedback variants relocate the LMO from the clients to the server: each client sign-compresses the residual between its momentum and its gradient estimator and sends $(\mathbf{s}_t^{(j)},\alpha_t^{(j)})$; the server aggregates, refreshes a global estimator, and applies the LMO. The additional per-layer scalar leaves the uplink at ${\approx}1$ bit per parameter, but the server-side quantity is now dense, being a scaled average of signs followed by the real-valued $\operatorname{polar}(\cdot)$ of that average, so the vote argument lapses and the full model must be broadcast unless a second error-feedback loop compresses it, which is the addition EF21-MuonSign makes. The complete procedures are Algorithms~\ref{alg:fed_workerlmo}--\ref{alg:fed_serverlmo}.


\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{What a controlled problem shows first.} A deterministic $L$-smooth convex quadratic (Appendix~\ref{app:images_task}) permits us to measure the scalar upon which the descent lemma turns: the alignment $\rho_t=\langle\nabla f,\mathbf{D}_t\rangle/(\|\nabla f\|_F\|\mathbf{D}_t\|_F)$, which Theorems~\ref{th:1}--\ref{th:3} drive negative by construction. On random instances it never becomes negative: all three placements keep $\rho_t$ well clear of zero, so the counterexamples are not a configuration ordinary data reproduces. That is direct evidence for reading them as worst-case rather than predictive, and it accounts for the network results below running contrary to them. The one method whose $\rho_t$ does dip negative is EF21-MuonSign, the one carrying a proof: error feedback converges through its estimator, not through per-step descent. Two readings carry forward. SignMuon and SignSGD emit steps of \emph{identical} Frobenius length, so whatever separates them is alignment, not step length. And every method here but SGD takes a norm-fixed step, which at a constant rate settles into a ball of radius $\eta\|\mathbf{s}\|_F$ rather than converging, so a fixed-target iteration count ranks accuracy \emph{floors}, not rates.

Rates are fixed a priori by the unit-gain rule at one $\eta_0$ per \emph{family} rather than tuned per method: every contrast is matched-hyperparameter, at the cost of not reporting each method's best loss. Three conclusions follow (Table~\ref{tab:nanogpt}, Figure~\ref{fig:nanogpt}). First, the matrix-aware 1-bit methods transfer to language modelling: every one of them improves on SignSGD by at least $0.08$ in validation loss, some eighty times the reference's seed standard deviation, while the best trail full-precision Muon by $0.008$, at equal wall-clock. Second, error feedback is inexpensive but not costless: the transition MuonUSign\,$\to$\,EF21-MuonUSign requires ${\sim}8\%$ more steps, the price of steering the LMO with an estimator that lags its target. EF21-SignMuon, whose target $\operatorname{polar}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_t)$ is orthogonal and hence isotropic, forfeits almost nothing and is the strongest compressed method here, the placement the theory condemns again finishing ahead.

Third, the bidirectional method's two models \eqref{eq:two_models} separate at this width. Its broadcast model $\mathbf{W}$, at which every gradient is evaluated, is indistinguishable from EF21-MuonUSign, so the compressed downlink is costless where training occurs; the server model $\mathbf{X}$, the iterate the guarantee bounds, instead settles ${\approx}2.2$ nats above it, a persistent offset rather than a divergence, which Appendix~\ref{app:nanogpt} localizes to the single zero-initialized $\mathtt{c\_proj}$ layer. This is the mechanism of Remark~\ref{rem:dimension} rather than a tuning failure: only a spectrally contractive downlink compressor eliminates it (Remark~\ref{rem:normmismatch}).

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}


A sign step and a spectral step are each an LMO, each sound alone; composed, they constitute an LMO for no norm, and the descent property is forfeited in the composition rather than in either factor. On explicit instances, minimal in shape, \emph{every} placement of the sign around the oracle converts the update into an ascent direction on a linear objective, at any step size and any momentum; and error feedback on the oracle's \emph{output} repairs none of them, its single shared magnitude being unable to track a target that may jump by a constant.

Compressing before the oracle rather than after it does succeed: error-feedbacking the gradient leaves the estimator pursuing a target that moves at the step size, and the resulting methods are exact instances of an existing framework, inheriting its $\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})$ rate at a one-bit uplink and, with a second loop on the model, in both directions. The second bit is nearly costless in practice but not in theory: a sign compressor contracts in the Euclidean geometry and in no layer norm, so the step size forfeits a factor in the layer rank, manifest as the separation of the two models at transformer width.

Theory and experiment order the methods oppositely: on every task we measure, the best compressed method is the placement we prove unrepairable, while the provably convergent variants trail it. A controlled problem indicates why: the alignment our constructions drive negative remains comfortably positive on random instances, so the guarantee insures against an event those problems never generate. What we preclude is a guarantee holding \emph{everywhere}, not one holding under a realistic condition, and the identification of that condition is the open question. Two narrower ones remain: the $(L^0,L^1)$ rate under a compressed downlink, and whether \emph{any} one-bit compressor is layer-norm contractive.
